Chapter 3

A Framework for System Design Interviews

A system design interview gives you a vague problem like "design a news feed" and about 45 minutes. This chapter gives you a simple 4-step method for those 45 minutes: understand the problem, sketch a high-level design, dig into the important parts, then wrap up. The method matters more than any single answer, because it is what the interviewer is really watching.

~30 min readBeginnerPrerequisites: Chapters 1–2 helpful, not required

The big picture

Imagine being asked to "design a well-known product X" in about an hour. Teams of hundreds of engineers built these products over many years. Nobody expects you to rebuild them in 45 minutes. So what is actually going on?

The book's key point is that a system design interview simulates real life. Two coworkers sit together to work on a vague problem and come up with a solution that meets their goals. The problem is open-ended. There is no single perfect answer. The final design matters less than how you got there.

That is good news for beginners. You do not need to memorise how Facebook works. You need a dependable process: ask good questions, propose something sensible, defend your trade-offs, and improve it with feedback. This chapter gives you that process.

Real-world analogy

Think of an architect meeting a client who says "I want a house." A good architect does not start drawing bedrooms. First come the questions: How many people live here? What is the budget? Will the family grow? Is the land on a hill? Then a rough sketch goes on the table: "two floors, kitchen here, garden there. Does that feel right?" Only after the client agrees does the architect work out plumbing and wiring for the tricky rooms. Finally the architect goes over the risks: "the basement could flood, so here is how we would handle it." That is exactly the 4-step framework: clarify, sketch, detail, review.

What the interviewer is really evaluating

Many people think the interview tests only technical knowledge. According to the book, it tests much more. The interviewer wants to see:

Technical design skills

Can you pick sensible components (load balancers, caches, databases) and explain why?

Collaboration

Do you work with the interviewer like a teammate, or lecture at them?

Working under pressure

Can you stay calm and organised when the clock is running and the problem is big?

Resolving ambiguity

When the problem is vague, do you turn it into something concrete by asking questions?

Asking good questions

Do your questions uncover the requirements that actually shape the design?

Making trade-offs

Can you explain what you gain and lose with each choice, instead of claiming one answer is "best"?

Red flags interviewers look for
  • Over-engineering. Many engineers love design purity and ignore trade-offs. Adding complexity the problem does not need costs money and time. The book notes that this kind of cost can add up for a real company.
  • Narrow-mindedness. Refusing to consider other approaches.
  • Stubbornness. Arguing with the interviewer instead of taking their feedback on board.

Key terms

Requirements
What the system must do. Functional requirements are features ("users can post"). Non-functional requirements are qualities ("fast", "always available", "handles 10 million users").
Design scope
The boundary of what you will design in this interview. Agreeing on scope stops you from wasting time on features nobody asked for.
Assumption
Something you decide is true so you can move forward, such as "assume posts are sorted newest first." Say assumptions out loud and write them down.
DAU (daily active users)
How many distinct users use the product on a typical day. It is the most common starting number for estimating scale.
Scale
How much load the system must handle: users, requests per second, data stored.
High-level design
A "big boxes and arrows" picture of the whole system: clients, servers, databases, caches and how they connect. Also called the blueprint.
Buy-in
Agreement from the interviewer that your blueprint is a reasonable direction before you go deeper.
Back-of-the-envelope estimation
Quick rough maths (Chapter 2) to check whether a design can handle the expected load, such as requests per second or storage per year.
API endpoint
A specific URL and method the client calls, such as POST /v1/me/feed, plus its inputs and outputs.
Database schema
The shape of the data: which tables or collections exist and what fields they have.
Deep dive
Zooming into one or two components to discuss their details, trade-offs and failure modes.
Bottleneck
The part of a system that limits overall performance, like the narrow neck of a bottle.
Single point of failure (SPOF)
A component that takes the whole system down if it fails.
Fanout
Sending one piece of data to many destinations. In a news feed, it means delivering a new post to all of the author's friends' feeds.
Trade-off
Gaining one benefit at the cost of another, such as faster reads in exchange for more storage.

The 4-step process at a glance

Every system design interview is different, but the book says a good one has common ingredients. It gives a 4-step process that works for almost any problem:

  1. Understand the problem and establish design scope. Ask questions. Agree on the features and the scale.
  2. Propose a high-level design and get buy-in. Draw the big boxes, check the numbers, ask "does this look right?"
  3. Design deep dive. Pick the most important components with the interviewer and work out their details.
  4. Wrap up. Discuss bottlenecks, errors, operations and future scale. Recap.
The 4 steps on a 45-minute timeline (one typical split)
The deep dive gets the most time. Scope and wrap-up are short but you should never skip them. The book says these numbers are rough guides. The real split depends on the problem and the interviewer.

Step 1: Understand the problem and establish design scope (3–10 min)

The book opens this step with a warning: do not jump right into a solution. In most interviews, answering fast earns no extra credit. Answering without fully understanding the requirements is a big red flag, because system design questions are open-ended on purpose.

The story of Jimmy

The book tells a story about Jimmy, a student who always raises his hand first. He answers before the teacher finishes the question. In school that might look clever. In a system design interview it backfires. Jimmy might end up designing the wrong system. The book's advice: slow down. Think deeply and ask questions to clarify requirements and assumptions.

Engineers like solving hard problems and jumping straight to a final design. That instinct hurts here. The book says one of the most important skills is to ask the right questions, make the proper assumptions, and gather all the information needed to build a system.

How it works

  • You ask a question. The interviewer either answers directly or asks you to make your own assumptions.
  • If you make an assumption, write it down on the whiteboard or doc. You may need it later.
  • Keep a visible list of requirements and assumptions so both of you can refer back to it.

What kind of questions to ask

The book gives a starter list. Each question exists because the answer changes the design:

Question (from the book)Why it matters to the design
What specific features are we going to build?Sets the scope. "Post and view feed" is a different system from "post, view, like, comment, search."
How many users does the product have?Drives every estimate: servers, storage, bandwidth. 1,000 users can run on one machine. 10 million cannot.
How fast does the company anticipate to scale up? What are the anticipated scales in 3 months, 6 months, and a year?Tells you how much room to leave for growth, and whether to plan for sharding or multiple data centers now.
What is the company's technology stack?You may need to fit into existing tools rather than choosing your own.
What existing services might you leverage to simplify the design?Reusing an existing auth, storage or notification service saves effort and simplifies your diagram.

Suppose you are asked to design a news feed system. Here is the kind of conversation the book shows, with notes on what each answer tells you:

Printable question bank: clarifying questions for any problem

The book's starter list is a good base. Below is a bigger bank, grouped by topic, that you can reuse for almost any problem. You will not ask all of them. Pick the 5–8 that matter most for the problem you are given. Spend 3–10 minutes, not 20.

1. Features and users

  • What are the 2–3 most important features?
  • Who are the users: consumers, businesses, internal teams?
  • Which clients: web, mobile, both, other services?
  • Is anything explicitly out of scope?

2. Scale

  • How many total users? How many daily active users?
  • How many actions per user per day (posts, reads, uploads)?
  • Is the system read-heavy or write-heavy?
  • Are there traffic peaks (events, time of day)?

3. Growth

  • Expected scale in 3 months, 6 months, 1 year?
  • Will we expand to new regions or countries?
  • Should I design for today's scale or for 10x?

4. Data

  • What kind of data: text, images, video, files?
  • Maximum sizes (post length, file size)?
  • How long do we keep data? Can it be deleted?
  • How are items related (friends, followers, groups)?

5. Quality requirements

  • What latency is acceptable for the main actions?
  • Is high availability more important than strong consistency?
  • Is it OK if a new post appears a few seconds late?
  • Any security, privacy or compliance needs?

6. Environment

  • What is the company's technology stack?
  • What existing services can we reuse (auth, storage, notifications)?
  • Single data center or multiple?
  • Any budget or cost constraints?
Tip: end Step 1 with a summary

Close the step with one sentence like: "So to confirm, we are building X with features A and B, for N daily users, and we care most about Y. Is that right?" This gets agreement and gives you a clean bridge into Step 2.

Step 2: Propose a high-level design and get buy-in (10–15 min)

Now you develop a high-level design and reach agreement with the interviewer on it. The book stresses that this is a collaboration. Treat the interviewer as a teammate and work together.

The book lists what to do in this step:

  1. Come up with an initial blueprint and ask for feedback. Many good interviewers enjoy talking things through and getting involved. Invite them in.
  2. Draw box diagrams with key components on the whiteboard or paper. These might include clients (mobile/web), APIs, web servers, data stores, cache, CDN, message queue, and so on.
  3. Do back-of-the-envelope calculations to check whether your blueprint fits the scale constraints. Think out loud. Check with the interviewer whether this estimation is needed before diving in.
  4. Go through a few concrete use cases if possible. This helps you frame the high-level design and may reveal edge cases you had not thought about.

Should you include API endpoints and the database schema?

The book says it depends on the problem:

Usually worth it

  • Smaller, focused problems. The book's example is designing the backend for a multiplayer poker game. Endpoints and schema are fair game there.
  • When the interviewer asks for them.

Usually too low-level

  • Very large problems. The book's example is "design the Google search engine." Talking about schemas there is too detailed for this stage.
  • When time is short and the big picture is not settled yet.

When in doubt, ask: "Would you like me to sketch the main API endpoints and the data model, or keep going at the component level?"

A quick back-of-the-envelope check

The book does not give numbers for this example. Here is how you would reason during the interview using Chapter 2 techniques. These figures come from assumptions you state out loud, not from the book:

Assume: 10 million DAU (given), each user opens the feed ~10 times a day (assumption)
Feed reads per second ≈ 10,000,000 × 10 / 86,400 s ≈ ~1,200 QPS
Peak (assume 2x average) ≈ ~2,400 QPS
=> Many web servers behind a load balancer, and a cache for feeds.
Why this matters

The point of the estimate is not exact numbers. It is to show the interviewer that your boxes are justified. "We read the feed far more than we write posts, so caching the feed makes sense" is a design decision backed by a rough number.

Step 3: Design deep dive (10–25 min)

The book says that by this step you and the interviewer should already have:

  • Agreed on the overall goals and feature scope.
  • Sketched out a high-level blueprint for the overall design.
  • Obtained feedback from the interviewer on the high-level design.
  • Had some initial ideas about which areas to focus on in the deep dive, based on that feedback.

Now you work with the interviewer to identify and prioritise components in the architecture. Every interview is different:

  • Sometimes the interviewer hints that they want to focus on the high-level design.
  • For a senior candidate, the discussion may be about system performance characteristics, likely focusing on bottlenecks and resource estimations.
  • In most cases, the interviewer wants you to dig into details of some system components.

It is easy to get carried away with tiny details that do not show off your abilities. Step 4: Wrap up (3–5 min)

In the last few minutes the interviewer may ask follow-up questions or give you freedom to discuss other points. The book lists several directions you can take:

Identify bottlenecks

The interviewer might ask you to find the system's bottlenecks and discuss improvements. Never say your design is perfect and nothing can be improved. There is always something. This is a chance to show critical thinking and leave a good final impression.

Recap your design

Give a short summary, especially if you suggested several solutions. It refreshes the interviewer's memory after a long session.

Error cases

What happens on server failure or network loss? Talking about these is interesting.

Operations

How do you monitor metrics and error logs? How do you roll out the system?

Next scale curve

How would you change the design to go from, for example, 1 million users to 10 million users?

Other refinements

Improvements you would make with more time.

Dos and Don'ts

Dos (from the book)

  • Always ask for clarification. Do not assume your assumption is correct.
  • Understand the requirements of the problem.
  • Remember there is neither the right answer nor the best answer. A solution for a young startup is different from one for an established company with millions of users. Make sure you understand the requirements.
  • Let the interviewer know what you are thinking. Communicate with your interviewer.
  • Suggest multiple approaches if possible.
  • Once you agree on the blueprint, go into details on each component. Design the most critical components first.
  • Bounce ideas off the interviewer. A good interviewer works with you as a teammate.
  • Never give up.

Don'ts (from the book)

  • Don't be unprepared for typical interview questions.
  • Don't jump into a solution before clarifying the requirements and assumptions.
  • Don't go into too much detail on a single component at the beginning. Give the high-level design first, then drill down.
  • If you get stuck, don't hesitate to ask for hints.
  • Again, communicate. Don't think in silence.
  • Don't think the interview is done once you give the design. You are not done until the interviewer says you are done. Ask for feedback early and often.

How to answer this in an interview: a full mock interview

I see two main flows: feed publishing, when someone posts, and news feed building, when someone opens their feed. I'll sketch each one. Would a quick traffic estimate be useful first?
Sure, keep it short.
I'll assume each user opens their feed about 10 times a day. 10 million times 10, divided by about 86,400 seconds, is roughly 1,200 feed reads per second on average, maybe double that at peak. Posting will be much less frequent. So this is read-heavy, which tells me caching the feed will pay off.
For publishing: the client calls POST /v1/me/feed with the content and an auth token. Requests go through DNS and a load balancer to stateless web servers. The web servers call three services. The post service saves the post to the post database and post cache. The fanout service pushes the new post into each friend's news feed cache. The notification service tells friends there's something new.
For reading: the client calls GET /v1/me/feed. It goes through the load balancer to the web servers, then to a news feed service, which reads the precomputed feed from the news feed cache. Because we did the work at publish time, reads are fast.
Why precompute feeds instead of building them when the user opens the app?
Reads are far more common than writes here, so I'd rather pay the cost once at write time than every time someone opens the app. The trade-off is extra work at publish time, especially for users with many friends. I'd like to look at that in the deep dive. Does this overall shape look reasonable to you?
Yes, that looks good. Let's go deeper.
3
Design deep dive
10–25 min
  • Let the interviewer help choose what to dive into.
  • Explain each step of the flow and the trade-offs.
  • Avoid rabbit holes such as ranking algorithms.
I'd suggest two areas: how fanout works when someone publishes, and how we retrieve and assemble a feed quickly. Which would you like to start with?
Start with publishing.
When a post comes in, the web servers first authenticate the user and apply a rate limit so one account can't spam posts. The fanout service then fetches the author's friend IDs, for example from a graph database. It uses the user cache to filter out friends who muted this person. Fanning out to up to 5,000 feeds is slow, so instead of doing it while the user waits, the fanout service puts the job on a message queue. Fanout workers pull jobs and append the post ID to each friend's list in the news feed cache.
What exactly do you store in the news feed cache?
Only small items, like a post ID and user ID, not the full post. Storing full posts and user profiles for every friend would use a huge amount of memory. I'd also cap each user's cached feed to recent posts, since most people only scroll through the latest content.
What about a celebrity with millions of followers?
Pushing to everyone would be very expensive. That's the classic hotkey problem. A common answer is a hybrid. For most users we push, as above. For accounts with huge followings we don't fan out. Instead, followers pull those posts when they load their feed and merge them in. That keeps writes manageable. For this problem the cap is 5,000 friends, so the push model mostly works, but I'd keep the hybrid in mind if followers are added later.
OK. Now retrieval.
The client calls GET /v1/me/feed. The news feed service gets the list of post IDs from the news feed cache. Then it hydrates them, fetching author names and profile pictures from the user cache and post content from the post cache, falling back to the databases on a cache miss. It returns the assembled feed as JSON. Images and videos are stored in object storage and served from a CDN, so the client loads them directly from a nearby edge server.
4
Wrap up
3–5 min
  • Recap in a few sentences.
  • Name bottlenecks yourself. Never claim the design is perfect.
  • Mention failures, monitoring and the next scale step.
We have a few minutes left. Anything you'd improve?
To recap: we split the system into publishing and retrieval. Publishing writes the post, then fans it out asynchronously through a queue into per-user feed caches. Retrieval reads post IDs from the cache and hydrates them, with media from a CDN.
On bottlenecks: the database will need scaling. I'd add read replicas, and later shard the post data. The news feed cache is a big memory consumer, so I'd watch its size. For failures: web servers are stateless, so losing one is fine behind the load balancer. Caches and databases need replication so they're not single points of failure. The queue lets workers retry if one crashes.
For operations, I'd monitor feed latency, fanout queue length, cache hit rate and error rates, with alerts. For the next scale curve, say from 10 million to 100 million users, I'd consider multiple data centers. With more time, I'd also discuss likes and comments, and a ranking model.

Common mistakes

Jumping straight to the solution

Drawing boxes in the first minute is the "Jimmy" mistake. You might design the wrong system, and you signal that you don't gather requirements. Always start with questions.

Over-engineering

Adding multiple data centers, five kinds of databases and machine-learning ranking for a 1,000-user app shows poor judgment, not skill. Match the design to the scale you agreed in Step 1.

Thinking in silence

If you go quiet for two minutes, the interviewer has nothing to evaluate. Narrate: "I'm weighing a cache here versus reading straight from the database..."

Going deep too early

Spending 15 minutes on the database schema before the overall design exists leaves no time for the rest. High level first, then drill down.

Rabbit holes

Explaining a ranking algorithm in detail (the book's example is EdgeRank) uses up time without showing system design skill. Ask yourself, "Does this show that I can design a scalable system?"

Being stubborn

If the interviewer pushes back, they are often hinting at something. Consider it seriously: "That's a good point. If we did X instead, we'd gain... but lose..." Defending a bad choice to the end is a red flag.

Saying "the design is perfect"

The book is explicit: never say this. Every design has bottlenecks. Naming them yourself is a strength.

Stopping when the diagram is done

You are not done until the interviewer says so. Keep asking for feedback early and often.

Test yourself

1. According to the book, what does a system design interview simulate?

Real-life problem solving where two coworkers collaborate on an ambiguous problem and come up with a solution that meets their goals. That is why collaboration and process count more than the final diagram.

2. Name three red flags the book mentions.

Over-engineering (loving design purity and ignoring trade-offs and cost), narrow-mindedness, and stubbornness. Each one suggests you would be hard to work with on a real team.

3. Why is answering quickly in Step 1 a bad idea?

System design questions are deliberately vague. Answering without clarifying requirements means you may design the wrong system. The book calls it a huge red flag. Speed earns no extra points here; understanding does.

4. What should you do when the interviewer tells you to "make your own assumptions"?

Make a reasonable assumption, say it out loud, and write it down. You will likely need it later, and writing it down lets the interviewer correct it early if they disagree.

5. List the five starter questions the book suggests for Step 1.

What specific features are we building? How many users does the product have? How fast does the company expect to scale (in 3 months, 6 months, a year)? What is the company's technology stack? What existing services can we use to simplify the design?

6. When should you include API endpoints and a database schema in Step 2?

It depends on the problem. For a focused problem like a multiplayer poker game backend, they are fair game. For a huge problem like the Google search engine, they are too low-level at this stage. If unsure, ask the interviewer.

7. What are the two flows of the news feed's high-level design?

Feed publishing (a post is written to cache and database and populated into friends' feeds) and news feed building (the feed is built by aggregating friends' posts in reverse chronological order).

8. Why does the book warn against explaining Facebook's EdgeRank algorithm in a news feed interview?

It is a time-consuming detail that does not show your ability to design a scalable system. Time management is essential in the deep dive: spend minutes where they show the skills the interviewer is evaluating.

9. Give four things you can discuss in the wrap-up.

Any four of: bottlenecks and improvements, a recap of the design, error cases (server failure, network loss), operational issues (monitoring metrics and logs, rollout), how to handle the next scale curve (such as 1 million to 10 million users), and other refinements you would make with more time.

10. What is a rough time split for a 45-minute interview?

Step 1: 3–10 minutes. Step 2: 10–15 minutes. Step 3: 10–25 minutes. Step 4: 3–5 minutes. The book stresses this is a rough guide; the real split depends on the problem's scope and the interviewer's requests.

Cheat sheet

  • The interview simulates two coworkers solving an ambiguous problem. The process matters more than the final design.
  • Evaluated: technical design, collaboration, working under pressure, resolving ambiguity, asking good questions.
  • Red flags: over-engineering, narrow-mindedness, stubbornness.
  • Step 1 (3–10 min): do not jump in. Ask about features, users, growth, tech stack and existing services. Write down assumptions.
  • Step 2 (10–15 min): blueprint with box diagrams, back-of-the-envelope check, concrete use cases. Get buy-in. APIs and schema only if the problem's size suits it.
  • Step 3 (10–25 min): prioritise components with the interviewer. Go deep on the critical parts. Avoid rabbit holes.
  • Step 4 (3–5 min): bottlenecks, recap, error cases, operations, next scale curve, refinements. Never call the design perfect.
  • News feed = feed publishing (post service, fanout, notifications) + news feed building (news feed service reading a cache).
  • Deep-dive checklist: scaling, single points of failure, caching, consistency, async work, protection, IDs, monitoring.
  • There is no single right or best answer. Startups and large companies need different designs.
  • Think out loud. Ask for hints when stuck. Ask for feedback early and often.
  • You are not done until the interviewer says you are done.
